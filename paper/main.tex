\begin{abstract}
DNN accelerators that run the same inference workloads for long periods age unevenly, because NBTI and HCI stress concentrate on the most heavily used compute and memory units. Runtime schedulers need per-component estimates of that stress, and ideally a forecast, to move work before damage accumulates. We model a 28-node accelerator (16 MAC clusters, 8 SRAM banks, 4 NoC routers) as a graph and train a Hybrid GNN--Transformer on labels produced by analytical NBTI/HCI/TDDB models driven by a roofline-style activity simulator. On a held-out test set of 150 synthetic snapshots the predictor reaches $R^2 = 0.996$ (MAE $0.0052$, versus $0.107$ for a mean predictor), and a 10-step trajectory head reaches $R^2 = 0.945$ (MAE $0.036$, versus $0.104$ for a persistence forecast). Using the predictor as a surrogate, NSGA-II searches layer-to-cluster mappings over peak aging, latency and energy and lowers predicted peak aging by 8.6--19.3\% relative to a single-cluster static mapping across five workloads. A PPO runtime controller trained for 960 environment steps improves during training but does \emph{not} outperform a fixed round-robin rotation policy under the same reward; we report this as a negative result. All data are simulated, all results come from a single seed, and the code, checkpoints and figure-generation scripts are released so that every number can be reproduced.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Inference accelerators in data centres run repetitive workloads: the same MAC clusters execute the same layers and the same SRAM banks hold the same weights for months. Stress therefore concentrates in the same places, and transistor aging follows. Negative Bias Temperature Instability (NBTI) shifts threshold voltages under sustained gate bias, Hot Carrier Injection (HCI) degrades drive current in frequently switching devices, and Time-Dependent Dielectric Breakdown (TDDB) eventually destroys the gate oxide~\cite{hill2022cmos,kim2023reliability}.

The learned aging models closest to this work target circuit timing. Bu et al.~\cite{bu2024multiview} predict path-level aging-aware delay with a multi-view graph model and report $R^2 = 0.981$ at $3.96\%$ MAPE; GNN4REL~\cite{alrahis2022gnn4rel} predicts path-delay degradation with graph neural networks; AaDaM~\cite{ebrahimipour2020aadam} models aging-aware cell delay with a feed-forward network. These models answer a signoff question for a netlist. A runtime scheduler instead decides which cluster runs which layer, so it needs estimates at the granularity of architectural components, and it benefits from knowing how those estimates will evolve.

This paper studies that setting in simulation. Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A component-level aging predictor (Hybrid GNN--Transformer) over an accelerator graph, trained and evaluated on simulator-generated labels, with comparisons against naive baselines on held-out data.
    \item A 10-step trajectory head on the same backbone, evaluated per forecast step and per workload.
    \item An NSGA-II mapper that uses the predictor as a surrogate for peak aging alongside latency and energy.
    \item A PPO runtime controller and a same-scale comparison with simple fixed policies, which in our budget the controller does not beat.
    \item A released, end-to-end pipeline in which every reported number and figure is regenerated from checked-in artifacts by a script.
\end{enumerate}

The data are synthetic and the aging models are not calibrated to a process. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} lists what this implies.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Learned aging and reliability models.} Bu et al.~\cite{bu2024multiview} combine spatial--temporal attention with graph attention for path-level aging-aware timing prediction. GNN4REL~\cite{alrahis2022gnn4rel} uses graph neural networks with PNA aggregation to estimate reliability-induced path-delay degradation without repeated aging-aware static timing analysis. AaDaM~\cite{ebrahimipour2020aadam} learns aging-aware cell-library delays with a feed-forward network. All three operate on netlists and timing paths. Our predictor operates on architectural components and on synthetic labels, so its accuracy numbers are not comparable to theirs; we compare only against naive baselines on our own data.

\textbf{Multi-objective mapping.} NSGA-II~\cite{deb2002nsga} remains a standard choice for search with two to four objectives. Differential evolution~\cite{storn1997de,das2016recent} and its variants~\cite{tang2015ide} are strong in continuous spaces; our chromosomes are integer layer-to-cluster assignments, for which NSGA-II with integer repair is a natural fit.

\textbf{RL for hardware management.} PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} is widely used for control problems with discrete actions. We apply it to remapping decisions over an aging-aware reward and compare it with simple fixed policies.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{00_summary.png}
    \caption{Overview of the released results: held-out MAE against naive baselines, PPO against fixed policies on the same scale, trajectory metrics, NSGA-II per-workload results with the released predictor, and the PPO training curve.}
    \label{fig:summary}
\end{figure}

The pipeline has five stages. A roofline-style analytical simulator turns a workload and a layer-to-cluster mapping into per-component activity. A feature builder converts activity into an 8-dimensional vector per node. The Hybrid GNN--Transformer maps the graph to a per-node aging score, and a trajectory head extends it to 10 future steps. NSGA-II uses the predictor as a surrogate to search mappings offline, and a PPO agent learns runtime remapping actions. Fig.~\ref{fig:summary} collects the main results reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Accelerator graph}
\label{sec:graph}

The accelerator is a directed graph $G = (V, E)$ with $|V| = 28$: MAC clusters $v_0,\ldots,v_{15}$ (16 clusters of a $16\times 16$ PE array), SRAM banks $v_{16},\ldots,v_{23}$, and NoC routers $v_{24},\ldots,v_{27}$. MAC cluster $m$ connects to SRAM banks $m \bmod 8$ and $(m+1) \bmod 8$; SRAM bank $b$ connects to router $b \bmod 4$; routers are fully connected. Every link is stored in both directions, giving $|E| = 92$. Each edge carries a weight and a latency (1/1 for compute--memory, 2/2 for memory--network, 4/3 for router--router). These attributes are stored with the graph but are \emph{not} consumed by the GCN and GAT layers in the current implementation.

\subsection{Per-node features}
\label{sec:features}

Each node has an 8-dimensional feature vector: (0) switching activity; (1) compute utilization (MACs only, zero otherwise); (2) for MACs, the workload's normalized total latency, for SRAMs the access rate, for routers the traffic; (3) a duty-cycle slot that in the current implementation repeats the switching activity; (4) a temperature proxy $\min(1, c_0 + c_1 u + c_2 z)$ with type-specific coefficients, where $z$ is normalized energy (latency for routers); (5) node type in $\{0,1,2\}$; (6) a binary flag for attention-based workloads; and (7) stress time $\log(\max(t,1))/\log(1.8\times 10^6)$, clipped to $[0,1]$. The temperature feature is a proxy, not a thermal simulation.

\subsection{Physics-based aging labels}
\label{sec:aging_models}

Labels combine three standard degradation models; the models are textbook forms~\cite{hill2022cmos}, not contributions of this work.

\textbf{NBTI:} $\Delta V_{th} = A (\alpha t)^n$ with switching activity $\alpha$, stress time $t$, $A = 0.005$ and $n = 0.25$.

\textbf{HCI:} $\Delta I_d / I_d = B\, J^m \sqrt{t}$ with $B = 10^{-4}$, $m = 0.5$, and $J$ proportional to activity times utilization.

\textbf{TDDB:} $F = 1 - \exp(-\exp(kE - \beta))$ with $k = 2.5$, $\beta = 10$, and $E$ proportional to activity times $V_{dd} = 0.8$\,V. This form has no time dependence and, for $E \le 0.8$, gives $F < 4\times 10^{-4}$, so TDDB contributes negligibly to the labels.

\textbf{Combined score:}
\begin{equation}
    s_i = 0.40\, \min\!\Big(\tfrac{\Delta V_{th}}{0.2},1\Big) + 0.35\, \min\!\Big(\tfrac{\Delta I_d/I_d}{0.1},1\Big) + 0.25\, F_i .
    \label{eq:aging_score}
\end{equation}
The weights were chosen by hand and not swept.

\textbf{Trajectory labels.} For forecast step $h = 1,\ldots,10$ the same model is evaluated at cumulative stress time $h\,t$ with switching activity scaled by $(1 + 0.02h)$ plus Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.01$). The trajectory targets are therefore a smooth, largely deterministic extrapolation of the current state; the forecasting task is easier than forecasting real, workload-dependent drift.

\subsection{Hybrid GNN--Transformer predictor}
\label{sec:model}

The predictor applies a linear input projection ($8 \to 256$) with ReLU; three GCN layers~\cite{kipf2017gcn}, each with batch normalization, ReLU and a residual connection; one 4-head GAT layer~\cite{velickovic2018gat} ($4\times 64$, concatenated) with batch normalization and a residual; and a 2-layer transformer encoder (4 heads, FFN width 1024) over the nodes of each graph, with sinusoidal positional encoding by node index and an outer residual. A 3-layer MLP ($256 \to 128 \to 64 \to 1$, ReLU, dropout 0.1) with a sigmoid produces $\hat{y}_i \in [0,1]$. The model has 1.89M parameters.

\subsection{Trajectory head}
\label{sec:trajectory}

The trajectory model reuses the backbone and concatenates the encoder features $\mathbf{h}_i$ with the (detached) current prediction $\hat{y}_i$:
$\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i^{\text{traj}} = \sigma(\text{MLP}([\mathbf{h}_i \,\|\, \hat{y}_i])) \in [0,1]^{10}$, with layer widths $[512, 512, 256, 10]$, GELU activations and dropout 0.1. It is trained with discounted MSE plus a variance-matching term,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{traj}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \gamma^k \big(\hat{y}^{(k)} - y^{(k)}\big)^2 + \lambda \,\big|\text{Var}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}) - \text{Var}(\mathbf{y})\big|,
    \label{eq:traj_loss}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma = 0.95$, $K = 10$ and $\lambda = 0.02$, the variance taken across nodes. The encoder is fine-tuned together with the head.

\subsection{NSGA-II mapping optimizer}
\label{sec:nsga}

A workload with $L$ layers ($L = 12$--$15$ for our workloads) is mapped onto $C = 16$ clusters by an integer chromosome $\mathbf{m} \in \{0,\ldots,15\}^L$. We minimize
$f_1 = \max_i \hat{s}_i + 0.3\,\text{Var}(\hat{\mathbf{s}})$ (predicted peak aging plus a balance penalty), $f_2$ = latency$/10^8$ cycles and $f_3$ = energy$/10^9$\,pJ, where $\hat{\mathbf{s}}$ is the predictor's output for the simulated activity of mapping $\mathbf{m}$ at a reference stress time of 360{,}000\,s. The population of 20 contains four structured seeds (all layers on cluster 0, round-robin, shuffled round-robin, perturbed round-robin) and 16 random individuals. Variation uses SBX crossover ($p_c = 0.9$, $\eta = 15$) and polynomial mutation ($p = 0.1$, $\eta = 20$) with integer rounding. A SHA-1 keyed cache avoids re-simulating duplicate chromosomes. Search runs for at most 20 generations and stops early if a hypervolume estimate (reference point $[1, 10^9, 10^9]$, computed over the population) improves by less than $10^{-6}$ relative for 8 consecutive generations.

We report the reduction in \emph{predicted} peak aging (without the balance penalty) of the lowest-peak Pareto mapping relative to the static mapping that places every layer on cluster 0.

\subsection{PPO runtime controller}
\label{sec:ppo}

The environment holds a current mapping. Each step it simulates the mapping, computes per-node aging with the physics label model of Eq.~\eqref{eq:aging_score} (the learned predictor is not used inside the environment in the reported run), and builds a 368-dimensional observation: aging vector (28), a heuristic 10-step forecast formed by adding increments $0.01$--$0.05$ to the current aging (280), workload embedding (16), mapping (15), episode progress (1) and per-node budget margin (28). Observations are normalized online with running statistics.

There are five actions: (0) hold; (1) move the first layer assigned to the hottest MAC cluster to the coolest one; (2) rotate every layer by one cluster; (3) move one layer (chosen by step index) by eight clusters; (4) apply the lifetime planner's rebalancing recommendation. The reward is
\begin{equation}
    r = \text{clip}\big(0.5\,\Delta_{\text{aging}} + 0.3\,b + 0.2\,\Delta_{\text{lat}},\, -2,\, 2\big),
    \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_{\text{aging}}$ and $\Delta_{\text{lat}}$ are the relative improvements in peak aging and latency over a round-robin reference mapping and $b = 1 - \sigma(\mathbf{s})/\mu(\mathbf{s})$ is a balance score. Episodes last 10 steps.

The actor--critic shares a trunk of two residual blocks (hidden width 128, orthogonal initialization with gain $\sqrt{2}$; actor gain 0.01, critic gain 1.0). PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} uses the clipped surrogate, GAE ($\gamma = 0.99$, $\lambda = 0.95$), clip $0.2$, value clipping, 4 epochs per update, minibatch 16, a learning rate decayed linearly from $3\times 10^{-4}$ to $3\times 10^{-5}$, an entropy coefficient annealed from $0.01$ to $0.001$, and early stopping of an update when the approximate KL exceeds $1.5 \times 0.03$. Training runs 30 iterations of 32 steps (960 environment steps).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\textbf{Accelerator.} 16 MAC clusters over a $16 \times 16$ PE array, 8 SRAM banks, 4 routers, 1\,GHz, 0.8\,V, 51.2\,GB/s DRAM and 512\,GB/s NoC bandwidth.

\textbf{Workloads.} Layer-level models of ResNet-50 (15 layers), BERT-Base (12), MobileNetV2 (13), EfficientNet-B4 (15) and ViT-B/16 (13). These are reduced representations built from each network's characteristic layer shapes, not full layer-by-layer models; attention score and softmax operations are not modelled.

\textbf{Dataset.} 1{,}500 synthetic snapshots, each a random mapping of a randomly chosen workload simulated at a stress time drawn uniformly from 3{,}600 to $1.8\times 10^{6}$\,s. A seeded random split gives 1{,}200 training, 150 validation and 150 test graphs (4{,}200 test nodes).

\textbf{Training.} AdamW (learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$), cosine annealing, batch size 16. The predictor trains for up to 60 epochs with early stopping on validation MSE (patience 16); the trajectory model for up to 75 epochs with early stopping on validation $R^2$ (patience 18). All runs use seed 42 on one NVIDIA RTX~3050 (4\,GB).

\textbf{Metrics.} For prediction: $R^2$, MAE, RMSE and Spearman's $\rho$ over all test nodes, against a mean predictor (the per-target test mean) and, for trajectories, a persistence forecast that repeats the first-step target. For NSGA-II: Pareto-set size, generations used and the predicted peak-aging reduction defined in Section~\ref{sec:nsga}. For PPO: total reward per 10-step episode.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} \texttt{scripts/verify\_results.py} reloads the released checkpoints, regenerates the dataset from its seed and recomputes every prediction metric; all twelve reported prediction metrics reproduce to within $2\times 10^{-6}$. \texttt{scripts/measure\_paper\_figures.py} produces every supplemental figure and the NSGA-II, inference and baseline numbers below without any training.

During training the mean episode reward went from $1.092$ in the first iteration to $0.982$ in the last, with a mean of $1.032$ and a best iteration of $1.460$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ppo}). The reward does rise during training: the last ten iterations average $1.200$ against $0.808$ for the first ten. Fig.~\ref{fig:ppo_baselines} puts this on the same scale as fixed policies evaluated in the same environment: over 20 seeded episodes, random scores 1.021$\pm$0.376, threshold scores 0.726$\pm$0.240, round-robin scores 1.574$\pm$0.124, hold (no-op) scores 0.726$\pm$0.240. PPO's mean training reward ($1.032$) is at the level of the random policy, and both its last-ten-iteration mean and its best iteration stay below always rotating. (Training rewards come from a stochastic policy on unseeded episodes, so this comparison is approximate.) We conclude that, with this reward and budget, PPO improved during training but did not learn a policy better than fixed rotation. Training was also too short to support any stronger claim: 960 steps is two to three orders of magnitude below typical PPO budgets. The released checkpoints do not include the observation-normalization statistics, so the trained policy cannot be re-evaluated deterministically; the released trainer now saves them.

\subsection{Model size}

The predictor has 1.89M parameters (7.2\,MB in FP32) and the predictor with trajectory head 2.42M (9.2\,MB). We do not report inference latency: our only measurements were taken on a heavily shared machine and are not representative.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Synthetic, uncalibrated data.} All labels come from our analytical simulator and textbook aging models with uncalibrated coefficients. There is no silicon or external validation. The prediction results show that the model fits the simulator.

\textbf{Small dataset and single seed.} 1{,}500 snapshots (150 test graphs) and one seed for every stage. We report no confidence intervals; differences between workloads in particular are small-sample estimates.

\textbf{Simplified labels.} TDDB is effectively zero under our parameters, so labels are driven by NBTI and HCI. Trajectory labels are a smooth extrapolation of the current state rather than simulated future workload behaviour.

\textbf{Model inputs.} Edge weights and latencies are not used by the graph layers, and feature 3 duplicates feature 0. Neither affects the validity of the reported numbers, but both are simplifications relative to the intended design.

\textbf{Reduced workloads.} Workloads are 12--15-layer abstractions of the named networks, without attention score and softmax operations.

\textbf{NSGA-II.} The population is small (20) and the hypervolume used for stopping is an approximation over the whole population. Reductions are measured on predicted, not simulated, aging and against a pessimistic single-cluster baseline.

\textbf{PPO.} The controller acts on physics-model aging with a heuristic forecast rather than on the learned predictor and trajectory head, it was trained for only 960 steps, and it does not beat a fixed rotation policy.

\textbf{No ablation.} We did not measure the contribution of individual architectural blocks or forecast horizons.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In simulation, a Hybrid GNN--Transformer fits component-level aging labels on an accelerator graph closely ($R^2 = 0.996$) and forecasts a 10-step trajectory with a third of a persistence forecast's error. As a surrogate inside NSGA-II it finds mappings that substantially lower predicted peak aging relative to single-cluster placement. A short PPO run did not produce a controller better than fixed rotation. The most important next steps are calibrating the aging models against silicon data, running multiple seeds with confidence intervals, measuring architecture and horizon ablations, and connecting the learned predictor to the runtime controller with a substantially longer training budget.

Code, checkpoints and figure scripts: \url{https://github.com/grizzleyyybear/adaptive-aging-aware-DNN}.
